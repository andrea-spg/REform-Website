A Life Cycle Assessment (LCA) measures the environmental footprint of a product across every stage of its existence — from the moment raw materials are pulled from the earth to the moment the product is discarded. The comparison below applies the same LCA framework to two products that solve the same problem ("move air across a desk"): a typical mass-produced desktop fan, and the REform ventilator built from a salvaged hair dryer.

The Scenario

Functional unit: One small desktop fan, used 2 hours/day during summer (≈ 90 days/year), with an expected service life of 4 years. This is the comparable usage profile for both products.

Lifecycle Stage-by-Stage

The five-stage breakdown below mirrors the standard cradle-to-grave LCA boundary set by ISO 14040.

01
Raw Materials
Virgin ABS plastic, virgin steel, virgin copper magnet wire, neodymium magnets — mined and refined.
Salvaged motor and switch already exist. Only printed parts (≈ 80 g PLA) and ESP32 board are new.
02
Manufacturing
Mass injection moulding and motor assembly in factory in Guangdong; ≈ 3.2 kg CO₂-eq per unit.
3D printing + laser cutting in a local makerspace; ≈ 0.4 kg CO₂-eq per unit.
03
Distribution
Ship container Shenzhen → Rotterdam, then truck to retailer, then car/parcel to home — ≈ 9 000 km total.
Files downloaded; components already at home; printed parts travel ≈ 50 km from local maker hub.
04
Use
≈ 35 W draw on AC mains; sealed motor cannot be lubricated when bearings dry out.
PWM-controlled, draws 6–18 W via USB; every part can be re-greased or replaced.
05
End of Life
Glued ABS housing breaks first; whole unit goes to e-waste; ≈ 75 % landfilled or incinerated.
Screws unscrew; salvaged motor returns to the parts pool; PLA is compostable in industrial facilities.

Conclusions

Across five of the six key LCA indicators, the REform Hair Dryer ventilator outperforms the market alternative. The build emits ≈ 70 % less CO₂, uses 72 % less virgin material, travels 180× less distance from origin to user, and costs the user 57 % less out of pocket.

Critically, end-of-life behaviour is reversed: where the market product becomes shredder feedstock, the REform build returns its motor and switch to circulation as donor components for the next iteration. This is the inner loop of the circular economy in practice — the design itself is what closes it.

The trade-off is the user's own time, which is also the means by which they gain a working knowledge of mechanics, electronics, and design — value that the market product cannot offer.
